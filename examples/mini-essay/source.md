# Mini Essay: Why Definitions Matter in Disagreement

People often appear to disagree when they are using the same word in different ways. A discussion about whether a rule is "fair," for example, may stall because one speaker means equal treatment while another means treatment proportionate to need. Before deciding which position is stronger, the participants must determine whether they are answering the same question.

A useful distinction is therefore needed between verbal disagreement and substantive disagreement. A verbal disagreement results mainly from different uses of an expression. A substantive disagreement remains even after the relevant terms have been clarified. Clarification does not guarantee agreement, but it tells us what kind of disagreement remains.

This distinction changes the order of good argument. When a dispute may be verbal, definition and clarification should come before the evaluation of reasons. Once the terms are stable, the participants can examine evidence and arguments without confusing two different questions. Conceptual clarification is therefore not a substitute for argument; it is sometimes a condition for knowing which argument needs to be made.
