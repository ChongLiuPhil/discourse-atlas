# Correlation, Confounding, and Mechanism

Researchers may observe that two variables change together. Such a correlation can motivate a causal hypothesis, but the observed association alone does not identify which variable, if either, produces the other.

One reason is confounding. A third factor can influence both measured variables and thereby generate an association even when neither measured variable directly causes the other.

Stronger causal evidence therefore requires designs that discriminate among these possibilities. Controlled interventions, natural experiments, temporal ordering, and mechanism-sensitive measurements can each reduce different sources of ambiguity.

The methodological conclusion is not that correlations are useless. They are often valuable for discovery and prediction, but causal claims require additional evidence whose form is matched to plausible alternative explanations.
